## Teacher notes
Aim: she talks about traditions, Canada's and Belgium's, leaves knowing what her year over there will look like, and has lines to ask about a tradition and to explain one of her own. Same shape as *Meet Ottawa*: the slides are props, tangents are welcome. Don't re-teach what Ottawa covered (Winterlude, the Skateway, BeaverTails, the Stanley Cup, *toque*); just point back to it. Her form shaped this lesson: she's looking forward to local traditions and winter sports; meeting the host family and classmates for the first time makes her a little nervous (hence the Thanksgiving table and the talk task); she likes pyjama parties, pancakes, animals and *The Hunger Games*; she wants confidence and a better accent; when she gets stuck she laughs and switches to French; and she asked to be corrected **only on the big things**. Listen for: articles with holidays (*the Christmas*, *at the Halloween*), prepositions (*at Christmas*, *on Christmas Day*, *on 11 November*), French *on* turning into *one*, adverb position (*we eat always*), the *th* in *Thanksgiving* and *thankful*, and *I'm agree*. As written this runs past an hour: skip the pumpkin regatta, the tree for Boston, December words, the polar bear dip or the groundhog if talk runs long. Remembrance Day is tomorrow, so protect Part 3.

## Slide: Today
@art: maple
@kicker: Lesson two
Six months over there means a whole season of holidays you've only seen in films. Today is the calendar, September to February.

- You'll know what's coming, month by month, and why Canadians care about it.
- You'll have lines to ask about a tradition and to explain one of yours.
- And by the end you'll be able to hold your own at a Thanksgiving dinner.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Say:** "Last time was the city. Today is the calendar: everything you'll celebrate, wear, eat or slide down between September and February."
- **Ask:** "Which Canadian or American holiday do you think you've seen most in films?"
- **Bring in:** it's 10 November, so people in Ottawa are wearing red poppies right now. We'll see why in Part 3.

## Slide: Asking and explaining
@kicker: Lines to steal
You'll be asked about Belgium as often as you ask about Canada. Here's how both sound.

| Asking about it | Explaining yours | Reacting |
|---|---|---|
| *Is that a thing here?* | *Back home, we…* | *Oh, that's cute.* |
| *What do people usually do for…?* | *It's a big deal in Belgium.* | *Wait, seriously?* |
| *Do you guys do anything for…?* | *It's a bit like…, but…* | *I'd love to try that.* |
| *How does it work?* | *Kids usually get…* | *That sounds amazing.* |

**Stuck?** *Hang on, how do you say…?* · *Give me a second.* Stay in English, even if the word you need only comes in French.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Do:** ask her each question from the first column about Belgium, and react with the third.
- **Note:** *you guys* is for any group, girls included. Very Canadian, very normal.
- **The stuck line** matters most: when she freezes, hand it to her instead of letting her switch to French.

## Slide: How to explain a tradition
@kicker: Lines to steal
French has one handy word, *on*. English needs a choice.

| French | English |
|---|---|
| *On mange de la dinde.* | *People eat turkey.* (anyone) · *We eat turkey.* (your family) · *You eat turkey.* (anyone, casual) |
| *On le fête le 6 décembre.* | *We celebrate it on 6 December.* |
| *On mange toujours des crêpes le dimanche.* | *We always eat pancakes on Sunday.* |

- **Not** *one eats turkey*: correct, but it sounds like a textbook.
- *always, usually, often, never* go **before** the main verb (*we always eat*), but **after** *be* (*it's always cold*).
- Holidays take no *the*: *at Christmas*, *at Halloween*, *on Christmas Day*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "What does your family always do on a Sunday?" Get one or two full sentences back.
- **Listen for:** *we eat always…*, *at the Christmas*.

## Slide: The Marathon of Hope
@art: flag
@kicker: Part 1 · Back to school
@stat: 5,373 km
In 1977, an 18-year-old student from British Columbia, Terry Fox, lost his right leg to cancer.

- In April 1980, he dipped his artificial leg in the Atlantic in Newfoundland and started running west, to cross the whole country. He called it the *Marathon of Hope*.
- He ran about 42 km every day, a full marathon, for 143 days.
- Near Thunder Bay, Ontario, the cancer came back in his lungs, and he had to stop. He died the next year, aged 22.
- Every September since, Canadians run in his name. Your school will hold its own Terry Fox Run; together, the runs have raised over 850 million dollars for cancer research.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Say:** "This is probably the first Canadian tradition you'll take part in, a few weeks after you arrive."
- **Ask:** "Is there someone like this in Belgium, someone every school knows about?"
- **His goal** was one dollar from every Canadian, about 24 million then. He reached it in early 1981.
- **Language:** *raise money*, *artificial leg*, *in his name*.

## Slide: Spirit Week
@layout: cards
@art: camera
@kicker: Part 1 · Back to school
Most high schools have a *Spirit Week*: five days of dressing up to show school pride.

- **Pyjama Day.** Come to class in your pyjamas. Nobody bats an eye.
- **Twin Day.** Dress exactly like a friend. A very good way to make one.
- **Jersey Day.** Wear a sports shirt, usually hockey. Ottawa's team is the Senators.
- **Crazy Hair Day.** Pretty much what it sounds like.
- **Pep rally.** The whole school in the gym, cheering for its teams. Loud.
- **School colours.** Every school has them, and a mascot to go with them.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "Which day would you go all out for?" She likes pyjama parties: she'd win Pyjama Day.
- **Twin Day** is a gift for making friends: *Want to be twins on Thursday?* Have her say it.
- **Language:** *school spirit*, *mascot*, *go all out*, *not bat an eye* (not be surprised at all).

## Slide: Thanksgiving comes first
@art: cow
@kicker: Part 2 · Autumn
@stat: October
- Canadian Thanksgiving is on the second Monday of **October**, not in November like in the US. The harvest finishes earlier this far north.
- Canadians like to point out that theirs came first: in 1578, the explorer Martin Frobisher held a thanksgiving meal in the Arctic, 43 years before the American one.
- Families gather over the long weekend for turkey, stuffing, mashed potatoes, cranberry sauce and pumpkin pie.
- At many tables, everyone says one thing they're thankful for before eating.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Go back to her guess** from the start.
- **Say:** "You'll be there for this one, about six weeks in. It's probably your first big dinner with the whole host family."
- **Ask:** "Is there a meal in Belgium where the whole family gets together?" · "What's on the table?"
- **Language:** *stuffing*, *leftovers* (turkey sandwiches for days), *long weekend*.

## Slide: At the table
@kicker: Lines to steal · Thanksgiving
The night you'll most want something ready to say.

- *Could you pass the potatoes, please?*
- *This is so good. Did you make it?*
- *I'm thankful for…* (when it's your turn around the table)
- *Just a little, thanks.*
- *I'm stuffed.* (completely full; casual and fine at home)
- *Can I help clear the table?*

**Thank** starts with the *th* sound: tongue between the teeth, then blow. Not *sank*, not *tank*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Do:** be the host family. Pass her things, offer her more, then go round the table: "What are you thankful for?"
- **Pronunciation:** *thank*, *thankful*, *Thanksgiving*, *third*. Overdo the tongue the first time; she said she wants to work on her accent.
- **Note:** *Can I help clear the table?* wins over any host family on the first night.

## Slide: Costumes over snowsuits
@art: castle
@kicker: Part 2 · Autumn
@stat: 31 October
- Halloween came to Canada with Irish and Scottish immigrants. Back home they carved scary faces into **turnips**; over here, pumpkins were bigger and easier.
- In Ottawa it's often around zero on Halloween, and sometimes it snows, so kids wear their costumes over their snowsuits.
- Kids go door to door shouting *Trick or treat!* A carved pumpkin outside means the house has candy.
- At school, the question for weeks is *What are you going as?* (*I'm going as…*)

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "What would you go as?" *The Hunger Games* is an easy win: Katniss is a classic costume.
- **Teenagers** dress up at school, go to parties, and plenty still go trick-or-treating with friends.
- **Fun, and true:** in 2018 the city of Bathurst, New Brunswick raised its trick-or-treating age limit from 14 to 16, with an 8 p.m. curfew.
- **Language:** *candy* (Canada) / *sweets* (UK), *carve*, *costume*.

## Slide: The pumpkin regatta
@art: raft
@kicker: Part 2 · Autumn
@stat: since 1999
- In Windsor, Nova Scotia, a farmer called Howard Dill spent years breeding bigger and bigger pumpkins. His *Atlantic Giant* seeds now grow pumpkins heavier than a small car.
- Every October, the town holds a **pumpkin regatta**: people hollow out a giant pumpkin, climb inside, and race it across a lake.
- Some paddle; some fit a little motor on the back. Plenty sink before the finish.
- Thousands come to watch from the shore.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min. Skippable.
- **Ask:** "Would you race? What would you call your pumpkin?"
- **Language:** *hollow out*, *paddle*, *sink / sank / sunk* (from the Ottawa lesson).

## Slide: Remembrance Day
@art: bell
@kicker: Part 3 · Tomorrow
@stat: 11 a.m., 11 November
- At 11 a.m. on 11 November, Canada stops for two minutes of silence. The First World War ended at that hour in 1918.
- From the end of October, most people wear a red **poppy**, pinned on the left, over the heart. Volunteers hand them out for a donation.
- Schools hold an assembly: a trumpet plays the *Last Post*, then silence.
- In Ottawa, after the ceremony at the National War Memorial, people walk up and leave their poppies on the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, until it's covered in red.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min. Slower, quieter tone.
- **Ask:** "What happens in Belgium on 11 November?" (It's a public holiday there; in Ontario, it's a school day.)
- **Belgium link:** the Last Post has been played at the Menin Gate in Ypres every evening since 1928. Has she been?
- **Language:** *Lest we forget* (so that we don't forget: old English, read at every ceremony), *wreath*, *pin*.

## Slide: In Flanders Fields
@art: tulips
@kicker: Part 3 · Tomorrow
@stat: May 1915
- John McCrae was a doctor from Guelph, Ontario, treating wounded soldiers near Ypres in the spring of 1915.
- On 2 May his close friend, Alexis Helmer, was killed. With no chaplain there, McCrae led the burial himself.
- The next day, looking at the poppies growing between the graves, he wrote a short poem: *In Flanders Fields*.
- The story goes that he wasn't happy with it and threw it away, and another officer picked it up. It was published in London that December, and it's why the whole country wears a poppy.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Say:** "The symbol every Canadian wears in November comes from a field in Belgium."
- **Ask:** "Did you learn about the First World War at school? Have you been to Ypres?"
- **If she asks:** McCrae died of pneumonia in France in January 1918. The spot where he wrote it, Essex Farm near Boezinge, is a cemetery Belgian school trips still visit.

## Slide: The first verse
@kicker: Part 3 · Listen and read
Every Canadian student hears this at least once a year. Listen first, then read it aloud.

> In **Flan**ders **fields** the **pop**pies **blow**
>
> Be**tween** the **cross**es, **row** on **row**,
>
> That **mark** our **place**; and **in** the **sky**
>
> The **larks**, still **brave**ly **sing**ing, **fly**
>
> Scarce **heard** a**mid** the **guns** be**low**.

- The bold syllables are the strong beats: da-**DUM**, da-**DUM**. English poetry, and English speech, lives on that rhythm.
- *Scarce* is old-fashioned for *hardly*. *Larks* are small birds that sing as they fly.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Do:** read it once slowly. Then she reads it; then together.
- **Listen for:** stress on the bold syllables, *fields* (not "fiels"), *the* with a proper *th*, and *row* rhyming with *blow*.
- **Correct only the big ones,** as she asked: pick one sound, not five.

## Slide: A tree for Boston
@art: tree
@kicker: Part 4 · December to January
@stat: 1917
- On 6 December 1917, two ships collided in Halifax harbour. One was full of explosives. It was the biggest man-made explosion the world had seen.
- About 2,000 people died, and the next day a blizzard hit the city.
- Boston sent a train of doctors, nurses and supplies, which pushed through the snow to get there.
- To say thank you, Nova Scotia sends Boston a giant Christmas tree every year. It's lit up in the city's main park, and Bostonians still know why.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min. Skippable.
- **Ask:** "If a city helped Belgium, what would you send every year?" (Ottawa still gets Dutch tulips, from last lesson.)
- **Language:** *collide*, *blizzard*, *supplies*.

## Slide: December words
@layout: cards
@kicker: Part 4 · December to January
- **Ugly Christmas sweater.** The tackier the better. Schools hold contests.
- **Secret Santa.** You draw a name and buy that person a small present. Very common in classes.
- **Eggnog.** A thick, sweet, cold drink of milk, eggs and nutmeg. People love it or hate it.
- **Tourtière.** A spiced meat pie from Quebec, eaten late on Christmas Eve.
- **Boxing Day.** 26 December: the biggest shopping day of the year, with huge sales.
- **Happy holidays.** What you write on a card when you don't know what someone celebrates.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min. Skippable.
- **Ask:** "Which would you try first?" · "What would you buy someone for Secret Santa?"
- **Her French:** *tourtière* and *réveillon* she'll recognise; English speakers in Ottawa use both words too.

## Slide: The polar bear dip
@art: lakes
@kicker: Part 4 · December to January
@stat: since 1920
- On 1 January, thousands of Canadians run into freezing lakes and seas, in swimsuits, on purpose. It's called a **polar bear dip**.
- Vancouver's has been held every year since 1920.
- Many people do it for charity, often in costume.
- Most are in the water for a few seconds, then run straight for the towels and the hot chocolate.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min. Skippable.
- **Belgium link:** Ostend has its own New Year's dive, the *Nieuwjaarsduik*. Has she done it, or would she?
- **Ask:** "What's your New Year's resolution going to be over there?"
- **Language:** *on purpose*, *resolution*, *freezing* (very cold, said about anything below about five degrees).

## Slide: Chess on ice
@art: trophy
@kicker: Part 5 · Winter
@stat: 20 kg
- In curling, two teams slide heavy granite stones down the ice towards a target, called the *house*.
- Two players sweep the ice in front of the stone with brooms, to make it go further and straighter. The captain, the *skip*, yells *Hurry hard!* when they need to sweep faster.
- Most top-level stones are cut from the granite of one tiny Scottish island, Ailsa Craig, home to no people and thousands of seabirds.
- Most of the world's curlers are Canadian, and plenty of high schools have a team.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "Would you rather be the sweeper or the skip?" · "Ever tried it?"
- **She loves animals:** Ailsa Craig is a bird sanctuary, with gannets and puffins.
- **She said winter sports:** Ottawa's curling clubs run learn-to-curl evenings; a good thing to ask the host family about.
- **Language:** *sweep*, *slide*, *Hurry hard!* (shout it together).

## Slide: Snow day
@art: canal
@kicker: Part 5 · Winter
@stat: −40
- At −40, Celsius and Fahrenheit meet: it's the same number on both scales. Ottawa rarely gets that cold, but with the wind chill it can feel like it.
- On mornings with a big storm or freezing rain, the school buses are cancelled. You find out early, online or on the radio.
- A **snow day** is what every student hopes for: no school, and everyone goes tobogganing.
- *Toboggan* comes from a Mi'kmaq word: a long wooden sledge, curled up at the front.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "Have you ever had a snow day in Belgium?" · "What's the coldest you've ever been?"
- **Her dog:** dogs in Ottawa wear little boots in winter, against the cold and the road salt. "Would your dog put up with boots?"
- **Language:** *freezing rain* (rain that turns to ice when it lands), *black ice*, *bundle up*.

## Slide: Maple taffy
@art: pastry
@kicker: Part 5 · Winter
@stat: 40 to 1
- It takes about 40 litres of maple sap to make one litre of syrup: the sap is boiled for hours until it's thick and sweet.
- At the end of winter, families go to a *sugar shack* in the woods to eat pancakes, beans and ham, all drowning in syrup.
- The best part: hot syrup is poured onto clean snow, left a few seconds to firm up, then rolled up on a stick. That's **maple taffy**.
- You'll find it at Winterlude in February, right next to the BeaverTails.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "What toppings go on your perfect pancakes?" She mentioned pancakes with lots of toppings.
- **Her French:** *cabane à sucre*, *tire sur la neige*. Sugar season is March and April, so Winterlude may be her best chance.
- **Language:** *sap*, *boil*, *sticky*, *drowning in*.

## Slide: Groundhog Day
@art: cat
@kicker: Part 5 · Winter
@stat: 2 February
- Every 2 February, towns across Canada wake up a groundhog, a large, round, sleepy ground squirrel, to ask about the weather.
- If he sees his shadow, there will be six more weeks of winter. If he doesn't, spring is coming early.
- Ontario's star is **Wiarton Willie**, a white groundhog. Nova Scotia has Shubenacadie Sam; Quebec has Fred la Marmotte.
- His record is about as good as tossing a coin.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min. Skippable.
- **Accent fun:** a groundhog is also a *woodchuck*. Tongue-twister: *How much wood would a woodchuck chuck if a woodchuck could chuck wood?* Slowly, then fast. The *w* is lips rounded, not a French *v* or *ou*.
- **Ask:** "Would you trust a groundhog or a weather app?"

## Slide: Now talk
@layout: talk
@art: chat
It's Thanksgiving dinner, and Jamie is your host family. He asks: *So, what do you guys do in Belgium?* Explain one Belgian tradition from start to finish, so a Canadian could picture it. Use *Back home, we…* and *It's a bit like…, but…*.

Then pick the three Canadian traditions from today you most want to try in your first months, and say why. Jamie will try to talk you out of one; don't let him.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 8 min.
- **Round one** is a rehearsal for her first host-family dinner, the meeting she said makes her a little nervous. Ideas if she needs one: Saint-Nicolas on 6 December, the Easter bells, the carnival in Binche, the New Year's dive in Ostend. React big with the lines from slide 3, and ask follow-up questions like a curious host (*Wait, who's Père Fouettard?*).
- **If she stalls,** hand her *Give me a second…* rather than letting her switch to French.
- **Round two:** push back ("Curling? Really? Over tobogganing?"). Listen for *I'd rather…*, *because…*.
- **Close:** one or two big mistakes only, as she asked; one specific thing she did well; then ask what she'd like next time. Her first pick in the form was the first day at school.
