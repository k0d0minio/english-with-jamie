## Teacher notes
Aim: she enjoys an hour of English about where she's going, talks more than you do, and leaves curious about Ottawa. No drilling today; the slides are props, and a good tangent beats the next slide. There's a little more here than an hour on purpose: every story slide stands alone, so skip any of them from the sidebar if a conversation runs long. Across the hour, listen for articles on place names (*the Canada*, *in the Ottawa*), *I have been there last year* (past simple for finished time), *actually* meaning *currently*, and the stress on Otta-WA. Don't correct mid-flow; jot things down and give her two at the end.

## Slide: Today
@art: maple
@kicker: Lesson one
No grammar test. This is an hour of talking about the place you're going to live.

- By the end, you'll know things about Ottawa that most people who live there don't.
- You'll have a handful of ways to guess and react in English, so a surprising fact never leaves you silent.
- And you'll have talked for an hour without thinking about it too much.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Say:** "No tests today. I want to hear what you already think about Canada, and then tell you some very strange stories about the city you're moving to."
- **Ask:** "Honestly, how are you feeling about the move, out of ten?"
- **Bring in:** it's October, so the maples around Ottawa are turning red right now. Canadians call the season *fall*, not autumn.

## Slide: What you already know
@layout: cards
@art: chat
- **Three words.** What comes to mind when you hear *Canada*? Don't think, just say them.
- **Something Canadian.** A singer, a series, a food, a film. Anything you've come across.
- **Ottawa, in your head.** What do you picture? "Nothing yet" is an honest answer.
- **An animal.** Which Canadian animal would you most like to see in real life?
- **Curious, or nervous?** One thing you want to find out, and one that worries you a little.
- **A guess.** How cold do you think it gets in January?

### Teacher

- **Say:** "There are no wrong answers here. I just want to hear you talk."
- **Follow up:** "Why that word?" · "Where did you come across it?"
- **Nudges if she's stuck:** Céline Dion (from Quebec, sings in French too), Drake, Justin Bieber, Ryan Reynolds, *Schitt's Creek*, *Anne with an E*, poutine, maple syrup. Animals: moose, beaver, black bear, loon; polar bears live far to the north, not near Ottawa.
- **Keep her January guess.** It comes back near the end.

## Slide: Guessing and reacting
@kicker: Lines to steal
Six quizzes are coming. Here's how people sound when they're not sure, and when they're surprised.

| You're guessing | It's surprising | It makes sense |
|---|---|---|
| *My guess is…* | *No way.* | *That makes sense.* |
| *I'd say about…* | *Wait, really?* | *Oh, I've heard of that.* |
| *No idea, but I'm going to say…* | *That's wild.* | *That's so Canadian.* |
| *It's either… or…* | *I had no idea.* | *I'd love to see that.* |

**I'd** is *I would*: the soft way to guess or to want. French says *je dirais*; English says *I'd say forty million*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Do:** read each line with real feeling. Overdo *No way* and *Wait, really?* so she hears the tune, then tell her something surprising about yourself and let her react with a line.
- **Pronunciation:** *I'd* is one syllable, like "ide". Saying *I would* in full sounds stiff.
- **Listen for, all lesson:** *I'm agree*. It's *I agree*.

## Slide: Canada, the answers
@layout: cards
@art: lakes
- **Just over 40 million people.** Canada is more than 300 times the size of Belgium, with fewer than four times the people. Most of them live fairly close to the US border.
- **More lakes than the rest of the world put together.**
- **H0H 0H0.** Letters to Santa go to this postal code, and Canada Post volunteers write back.
- **$18 million of maple syrup.** Stolen from a warehouse in Quebec in 2011–12. Quebec makes most of the world's supply.
- **Hawaiian pizza** was invented in Ontario in 1962, by a Greek-Canadian restaurant owner. People still argue about it.
- **Loonie and toonie.** The $1 coin has a loon, a water bird, on it, hence *loonie*. The $2 coin is a *toonie*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "Which one surprised you most?" · "Would you eat Hawaiian pizza?" (*To be honest…*, *I'd say…*)
- **H0H 0H0:** every Canadian postal code goes letter-number-letter, number-letter-number; she'll need to say hers.
- **The heist** inspired a TV series, *The Sticky*.
- **Pronunciation:** *Quebec* in English is "kuh-BECK" or "kwuh-BECK".

## Slide: Colonel By's ditch
@art: locks
@kicker: Part 1 · Bytown
@stat: 1826–1832
In 1826 a British engineer, Lieutenant-Colonel John By, arrived in the forest to dig a canal 200 km long, from the Ottawa River to Lake Ontario.

- It was a military plan: if the Americans attacked, British boats could avoid the border.
- Thousands of Irish and French-Canadian workers built it, by hand, through swamps. Hundreds died of malaria.
- The workers' camp grew into a town, named after him: **Bytown**.
- The war never came. Today the canal is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and boats still go through its locks, opened by hand.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Say:** "Every city starts somewhere. Ottawa started as a camp of canal diggers."
- **Explain a lock** with your hands: a box of water that lifts a boat up a step. The drawing shows one.
- **Ask:** "Would you rather have been an engineer or a digger? Neither?"
- **Word:** *malaria* in a Canadian swamp surprises people. The French *paludisme* is no help here; English uses the Italian word.

## Slide: The strongest man in Bytown
@art: logs
@kicker: Part 1 · Bytown
@stat: 1 vs 150
Bytown was a rough lumber town: thousands of men cut trees and floated them down the river to sell.

- **Jos Montferrand** was a French-Canadian logger, famous for his strength and for standing up for French-Canadian workers.
- The legend says he once fought off 150 men on the bridge to Hull, alone.
- His party trick: a backflip in a tavern that left his boot prints on the ceiling. People came from miles to see the marks.
- He became a folk hero, *Big Joe Mufferaw*, with a song about him that Canadians still know.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "Do you believe the 150 men? What's the real number, do you think?"
- **Language:** this is a *legend* or a *tall tale*. Teach *tall tale*: a story everyone knows is exaggerated.
- **Belgium link:** does she know a Belgian folk hero? Tijl Uilenspiegel, Manneken Pis? Let her tell you.
- **Context, if she asks:** the Shiners were Irish gangs; the fights between Irish and French-Canadian workers lasted years. Keep it light.

## Slide: A prince on a log slide
@art: raft
@kicker: Part 1 · Bytown
@stat: 1860
Logs couldn't go over the Chaudière Falls, so Bytown built a wooden slide beside them, a water chute for timber.

- In 1860, the Prince of Wales (later King Edward VII) came to visit. Someone had an idea: put the prince on the slide.
- They built a raft of logs, the prince climbed on, and shot down the chute while thousands cheered from the shore.
- Hundreds of canoes were waiting at the bottom.
- He loved it, and said it was too short. After that, every important visitor was invited to *run the slide*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Say:** "Imagine doing that to a royal visitor today."
- **Ask:** "Who would you put on the slide now? A singer, a politician, a teacher?"
- **Also on that trip:** he laid the first stone of the Parliament buildings.
- **Idiom bridge:** it was a *thrill*; he was *thrilled*. A good word for her first winter on the canal.

## Slide: Why Ottawa?
@art: parliament
@kicker: Part 1 · Bytown
@stat: 1857
- Bytown was renamed **Ottawa** in 1855, from an Algonquin word meaning *to trade*.
- In 1857 Queen Victoria had to pick a capital. Toronto, Kingston, Montreal and Quebec City all wanted it.
- She chose the rough lumber town: right on the line between English-speaking Ontario and French-speaking Quebec, and harder for an American army to reach.
- In 1867 Canada became a country, and Ottawa was its capital.
- Say it like a local: **OT-ta-wa**, stress on the first part, and the middle *t* goes soft *(sounds like "AH-duh-wuh")*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "If you were the Queen, which would you pick, and why?" (*I'd pick… because…*)
- **Drill, lightly:** *Ottawa* (AH-duh-wuh), *Rideau* (ree-DOH), *Gatineau* (GAT-in-oh in English; locals use both).
- **Her French:** cross the bridge to Gatineau, Quebec, and French is the language of the street. More than a third of people in Ottawa can speak French too.
- **Listen for:** French stress at the end, *Otta-WA*.

## Slide: The night Parliament burned
@art: bell
@kicker: Part 2 · Parliament Hill
@stat: 3 February 1916
- On a winter evening in 1916, while politicians were debating, a fire started in the reading room.
- It spread through the building in minutes. Seven people died.
- In the tower, the great bell kept ringing the hours as the building burned. Around midnight, it crashed to the ground.
- The library, full of the country's books, survived: the librarian sent a messenger to shut its heavy iron doors just in time.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Say it slowly, like a story.** Pause before "around midnight".
- **Ask:** "If your house was on fire and you could save one object, what would it be?" (Classic, but it works.)
- **Language:** *burn down* (the building burned down), *survive*, *just in time*.
- **The library** you can still visit today is the one that survived. It's round, and beautiful.

## Slide: Parliament in a museum
@art: dino
@kicker: Part 2 · Parliament Hill
@stat: 1916–1920
- With no building, Parliament moved into the natural history museum down the road. For four years, the country was run from a museum.
- The museum had problems of its own: it was sinking into soft clay, and its stone tower had been taken down the year before.
- Today it's the **Canadian Museum of Nature**, full of dinosaur skeletons, with a glass tower where the stone one used to be.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "Where would you put a government if you had to move it tomorrow? A school? A shopping centre?"
- **She loves animals:** the museum has whales, birds, and a live-insect room. Worth a Saturday.
- **Word:** *sink / sank / sunk*. "The building was sinking."

## Slide: The cats of Parliament Hill
@art: cat
@kicker: Part 2 · Parliament Hill
@stat: 1924–2013
- In 1924, cats were brought into Parliament to catch rats and mice in the basement.
- They had kittens, lots of them, and were moved outside. But they stayed.
- Volunteers built them little wooden houses behind the buildings and fed them every day for decades. One man, known as the *Catman of Parliament Hill*, looked after them for over twenty years.
- In 2013 the last four cats were adopted into homes, and the cat sanctuary closed.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min. She'll probably want more; let her.
- **Ask:** "Cats or dogs?" · "Do you have animals at home? What are they like?"
- **Language:** *adopt* an animal, *look after*, *a stray* (a cat with no home).
- **Fun:** tourists used to visit the cats as much as the politicians.

## Slide: A flag with a 100-year queue
@art: flag
@kicker: Part 2 · Parliament Hill
@stat: 100+ years
- The flag on top of the Peace Tower, Parliament's tall clock tower, is changed every working day.
- Any Canadian can ask to be given one of the used flags, for free.
- So many people have asked that the waiting list is now more than a hundred years long. People sign up for their grandchildren.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "Would you join the queue? What would you do with the flag?"
- **Language:** *queue* in British English, *line* in Canadian: "a hundred-year line" sounds just as funny.
- **Bridge:** in Canada people say *stand in line*; she'll hear it at every shop.

## Slide: The photo and the cigar
@art: camera
@kicker: Part 3 · Spies, stars and visitors
@stat: 1941
- In the middle of the Second World War, Winston Churchill came to Ottawa and gave a famous speech to Parliament.
- Afterwards, a young Ottawa photographer, Yousuf Karsh, had a few moments to take his portrait.
- Churchill wouldn't put down his cigar. So Karsh walked up and took it out of his mouth.
- Churchill glared at the camera, and Karsh took the picture. It became one of the most famous photographs ever taken, and it's on the British five-pound note.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "Would you have dared?" · "Have you ever taken a photo you were proud of?"
- **Language:** *glare* (an angry look), *dare* ("I wouldn't dare").
- **Fun:** look up the photo together if there's a screen handy. The scowl is the whole story.
- **Karsh** was an Armenian refugee who arrived in Canada as a teenager and became a famous portrait photographer.

## Slide: The man who started the Cold War
@art: secret
@kicker: Part 3 · Spies, stars and visitors
@stat: 1945
- In September 1945, a young clerk at the Soviet embassy in Ottawa, Igor Gouzenko, walked out with over a hundred secret documents hidden under his shirt. They proved the Soviet Union was spying on its allies.
- Nobody believed him. A newspaper sent him away; so did the government.
- That night, men from the embassy broke into his flat. He and his family were hiding next door, with the neighbours.
- Many historians say this is where the Cold War began. For the rest of his life, he appeared on television with a hood over his head.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "If someone walked into a newspaper today with secret papers, would they believe him?"
- **Language:** *defect* (leave your country's side), *spy* (noun and verb), *hide / hid / hidden*.
- **The Cold War** in one line, if she needs it: forty-odd years of the USA and the Soviet Union being enemies without fighting directly.

## Slide: A bunker for the end of the world
@art: bunker
@kicker: Part 3 · Spies, stars and visitors
@stat: 4 floors down
- In the Cold War, people feared a nuclear war. So outside Ottawa, the government built a secret shelter four floors underground.
- It had offices, a hospital, a radio studio and a vault for the Bank of Canada's gold, so the country could keep running after an attack.
- People nicknamed it the *Diefenbunker*, after the prime minister, John Diefenbaker.
- It was never needed. Today it's a museum, and you can walk down into it.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "Would you go in? Does it sound exciting or creepy?" Teach *creepy*.
- **Pronunciation:** *bunker* rhymes with *junk-er*, not "boon-ker".
- **She can visit:** it's about 30 minutes west of the city. A good host-family day out.

## Slide: The president's sore back
@art: tree
@kicker: Part 3 · Spies, stars and visitors
@stat: 1961
- When world leaders visit Canada, they plant a tree in the gardens of Rideau Hall, home of the Governor General.
- In 1961, President John F. Kennedy and his wife, Jackie, came to plant a red oak.
- He took the spade, dug up a few spadefuls of earth, and felt something go in his back.
- It hurt for weeks. The tree is still there, and it's much bigger now.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "If you planted a tree somewhere famous, where would it be?"
- **Language:** *I hurt my back*, *something went in my back* (very natural), *sore*.
- **Fun:** the gardens are open to the public; she can go and find the tree.

## Slide: The hotel and the Titanic
@art: castle
@kicker: Part 3 · Spies, stars and visitors
@stat: 1912
- Next to Parliament stands the **Château Laurier**, a hotel that looks like a French castle.
- It was built by a railway company. The company's president, Charles Melville Hays, was sailing home from Europe for the grand opening.
- He was on the **Titanic**. He died when it sank, eleven days before the party.
- The opening was cancelled. Six weeks later the hotel quietly opened its doors, and Sir Wilfrid Laurier, the former prime minister it was named after, signed the guest book as its first guest.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "Have you seen *Titanic*? Did you cry?" (An easy, fun one.)
- **Myth to debunk:** people say the hotel's furniture went down with the ship. Historians found no evidence. Teach *myth* and *debunk*.
- **Language:** *grand opening*, *cancelled* (British spelling, two l's; Canada usually writes *canceled*).

## Slide: The Obamatail
@art: pastry
@kicker: Part 4 · Food, money and oddities
@stat: 2009
- A *BeaverTail* is fried dough, stretched flat like a beaver's tail, covered in cinnamon sugar. The classic one comes with a squeeze of lemon.
- In 2009, President Barack Obama stopped at the stand in the ByWard Market, Ottawa's old market, and bought one.
- The stand renamed it the *Obamatail* in his honour.
- In winter, there are BeaverTails stands right on the frozen canal.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "What's a sweet thing from Belgium you'd make a Canadian try?" (Waffles, speculoos, chocolate: she's the expert here.)
- **Language:** *fried*, *stretched*, *topping* ("What topping would you choose?").
- **The ByWard Market** is one of the oldest markets in Canada, from the 1820s. Good for a weekend wander.

## Slide: The coin you can't lift
@art: coin
@kicker: Part 4 · Food, money and oddities
@stat: 100 kg
- Canada's coins are made at the **Royal Canadian Mint**, in Ottawa.
- In 2007, the Mint made a gold coin half a metre across, weighing 100 kilos. Its face value is one million dollars; the gold is worth far more.
- In 2017, thieves climbed into a museum in Berlin with a ladder and stole one.
- It was never found. Police think it was cut up and sold.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "How would you carry a 100-kilo coin out of a museum?" Let her plan the heist; lots of conditionals for free (*I'd…, then I'd…*).
- **Language:** *heist*, *face value*, *worth*.
- **You can tour the Mint** in Ottawa and hold a real gold bar.

## Slide: Maman
@art: spider
@kicker: Part 4 · Food, money and oddities
@stat: over 9 m
- Outside the National Gallery stands a bronze spider more than nine metres tall, carrying a bag of marble eggs.
- Her name is *Maman*. The artist, Louise Bourgeois, grew up in France and made her as a tribute to her own mother.

- People walk right under her legs to get to the gallery.

### Teacher

## Slide: A farm in the middle of the city
@art: cow
@kicker: Part 4 · Food, money and oddities
@stat: since 1886
- In the middle of Ottawa, between the houses and the offices, there's a working farm: the **Central Experimental Farm**.
- It has cows, horses, sheep, pigs, goats and chickens, and fields where scientists test new crops.
- You can visit the barns, watch the cows being milked, and in spring, see the new-born animals.
- Ottawa says it's the only capital city in the world with a working farm inside it.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "Would you rather live on a farm or in a city?" · "Which farm animal would you take home?"
- **She loves animals:** this is her place. The spring babies are worth planning around, if she's still there.
- **Language:** *crops*, *barn*, *milking*.

## Slide: The princess and the tulips
@art: tulips
@kicker: Part 4 · Food, money and oddities
@stat: 1943
- In the Second World War, part of the Dutch royal family escaped to Ottawa.
- When Princess Margriet was born in 1943, Canada declared her hospital room *not Canada* for the day, so she would be born only Dutch.
- After the war, the Netherlands sent Ottawa thousands of tulip bulbs to say thank you. They still send them every year.
- Each May, a million tulips flower across the city for the Tulip Festival.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Belgium link:** Canadian soldiers fought to free Belgium and the Netherlands in 1944. The tulips are part of the same thank-you.
- **Ask:** "If you could thank a country with a present, what would Belgium send?"
- **Heads up:** the Tulip Festival is in May, after she's home. She'll see the bulbs planted in autumn, though.

## Slide: The Skateway
@art: canal
@kicker: Part 5 · Winter
@stat: 7.8 km
- Every winter, 7.8 km of the Rideau Canal freezes into the world's biggest natural skating rink.
- People skate to work on it, with their bags on their backs, and stop for hot chocolate and BeaverTails on the ice.
- It only opens when the ice is thick enough. In the winter of 2022–23, it didn't open at all.
- In February, the **Winterlude** festival fills it with people, ice sculptures and snow slides.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 3 min.
- **Ask:** "Have you ever skated?" · "Would you skate to school if you could?"
- **Go back to her January guess** from the start: January sits around −10°C, and the wind can make it feel like −30. "It's minus thirty with the wind chill" is on the radio every winter morning.
- **Words she'll need:** *toque* (a woolly hat; rhymes with *spook*; she could knit her own), *mitts*, *boots*.

## Slide: The Cup in the canal
@art: trophy
@kicker: Part 5 · Winter
@stat: 1905
- In 1892, Lord Stanley, the Governor General, bought a silver bowl in London as a prize for the best hockey team in Canada. It's now the **Stanley Cup**, the most famous trophy in hockey.
- His children played hockey on an outdoor rink at Rideau Hall.
- In 1905, Ottawa's team won the Cup. After the party, the story goes, one of the players kicked it into the frozen canal, and they went back for it the next morning.
- Did it really happen? Some of the players' families said no. Nobody can prove it either way.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 2 min.
- **Ask:** "Do you believe it? Why?" Good for hedging: *I doubt it*, *It sounds like…*, *I'd believe it*.
- **Hockey in Canada** means ice hockey. Ottawa's team is the Senators, the *Sens*. A game with the host family is a classic first-month outing.
