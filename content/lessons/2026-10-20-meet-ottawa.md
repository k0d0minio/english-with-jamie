## Teacher notes
Aim: she enjoys an hour of English about where she's going, talks more than you do, and leaves curious about Ottawa. No drilling today; the slides are props, and a good tangent beats the next slide. Each slide has its own notes (what to say, what to ask, timing). Across the hour, listen for articles on place names (*the Canada*, *in the Ottawa*), *I have been there last year* (past simple for finished time), *actually* meaning *currently*, and the stress on Otta-WA. Don't correct mid-flow; jot things down and give her two at the end.

## Slide: Guessing and reacting
@kicker: Lines to steal
Two quizzes are coming. Here's how people sound when they're not sure, and when they're surprised.

| You're guessing | It's surprising | It makes sense |
|---|---|---|
| *My guess is…* | *No way.* | *That makes sense.* |
| *I'd say about…* | *Wait, really?* | *Oh, I've heard of that.* |
| *No idea, but I'm going to say…* | *That's wild.* | *That's so Canadian.* |
| *It's either… or…* | *I had no idea.* | *I'd love to see that.* |

**I'd** is *I would*: the soft way to guess or to want. French says *je dirais*; English says *I'd say forty million*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 5 min.
- **Do:** read each line with real feeling. Overdo *No way* and *Wait, really?* so she hears the tune, then tell her something surprising about yourself and let her react with a line.
- **Ask:** "Which of these would you never say?" It gets a laugh and a real opinion.
- **Pronunciation:** *I'd* is one syllable, like "ide". Saying *I would* in full sounds stiff.
- **Listen for:** *I'm agree* when she reacts. It's *I agree*.

## Slide: Canada, the answers
@layout: cards
@art: lakes
- **Just over 40 million people.** Canada is more than 300 times the size of Belgium, with fewer than four times the people. Most of them live fairly close to the US border.
- **More lakes than the rest of the world put together.**
- **H0H 0H0.** Letters to Santa go to this postal code, and Canada Post volunteers write back.
- **$18 million of maple syrup.** Stolen from a warehouse in Quebec in 2011–12. Quebec makes most of the world's supply.
- **Hawaiian pizza** was invented in Ontario in 1962, by a Greek-Canadian restaurant owner. People still argue about it.
- **Loonie and toonie.** The $1 coin has a loon, a water bird, on it, hence *loonie*. The $2 coin is a *toonie*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 5 min.
- **Ask:** "Which one surprised you most?" · "Would you eat Hawaiian pizza?" Good for opinions: *To be honest…*, *I'd say…*
- **H0H 0H0** reads "ho ho ho". Every Canadian postal code goes letter-number-letter, number-letter-number; she'll need to say hers.
- **The heist:** the thieves drained the syrup from the barrels and refilled them with water. It inspired a TV series, *The Sticky*.
- **Pronunciation:** *Quebec* in English is "kuh-BECK" or "kwuh-BECK".

## Slide: Why Ottawa?
@art: parliament
- In 1857 Queen Victoria had to pick a capital. Toronto, Kingston, Montreal and Quebec City all wanted it.
- She chose a rough lumber town between them: right on the line between English-speaking Ontario and French-speaking Quebec, and harder for an American army to reach.
- Its old name was **Bytown**. *Ottawa* comes from an Algonquin word meaning *to trade*.
- Cross a bridge over the river and you're in **Gatineau, Quebec**, where French is the language of the street. Your French will come in handy.
- Say it like a local: **OT-ta-wa**, stress on the first part, and the middle *t* goes soft *(sounds like "AH-duh-wuh")*.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 6 min.
- **Say it as a story:** "Imagine it's 1857. Four cities are fighting to be the capital…"
- **Ask:** "If you were the Queen, which would you pick, and why?" (*I'd pick… because…*)
- **Drill, lightly:** *Ottawa* (AH-duh-wuh), *Rideau* (ree-DOH), *Gatineau* (GAT-in-oh in English; locals use both).
- **Her French:** more than a third of people in Ottawa can speak French, but English comes first on the Ontario side. In Gatineau it flips.
- **Listen for:** French stress at the end, *Otta-WA*. Locals will hear it straight away.

## Slide: Ottawa's strangest stories
@layout: cards
@art: tulips
- **The princess.** In the Second World War, part of the Dutch royal family lived in Ottawa. When Princess Margriet was born in 1943, her hospital room was declared outside Canada so she would be born only Dutch. The Netherlands still sends tulips every year to say thank you.
- **The cats.** For decades, stray cats lived on Parliament Hill in little wooden houses built by volunteers. The last ones were adopted in 2013.
- **The Obamatail.** A *BeaverTail* is fried dough stretched flat like a beaver's tail, with cinnamon sugar on top. When Obama visited in 2009, the stand renamed it after him.
- **The library.** In 1916 the Parliament building burned down. The library survived because someone shut its heavy iron doors in time.
- **The spider.** A bronze spider over nine metres tall stands outside the National Gallery. Her name is *Maman*.
- **The bunker.** Just outside the city is a four-storey underground bunker, built for the government during the Cold War in case of nuclear war. Now it's a museum.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 8 min. This is the main speaking moment.
- **Do:** she picks the story she'd tell her friends first and retells it in her own words, without looking. Then: "Which would you visit first?"
- **The cats:** she loves animals; let this one run.
- **Belgium link:** Canadian soldiers fought to free Belgium and the Netherlands in 1944. The tulips are part of the same thank-you.
- **Heads up:** the Tulip Festival is in May, after she's home. Winterlude, in February, she'll catch.

## Slide: Ottawa in winter
@art: canal
- **The Skateway.** 7.8 km of the Rideau Canal freezes into the world's biggest natural skating rink, and people skate to work on it.
- The canal was finished in 1832, in case of war with the Americans. Nobody planned the skating.
- January sits around −10°C, and the wind can make it feel like −30. One of the coldest capitals in the world, though Ulaanbaatar, in Mongolia, is colder.
- **Winterlude**, in February: ice sculptures, snow slides, and BeaverTails on the ice.
- You'll want a **toque** (a woolly hat; you could knit your own), proper boots and mitts.

### Teacher
- **Time:** 5 min.
- **Go back to her January guess** from slide 2. Close?
- **Ask:** "What's the coldest you've ever been?" · "Have you ever skated?"
- **Words:** *toque* rhymes with *spook*. *Wind chill*: "It's minus thirty with the wind chill" is on the radio every winter morning.
- **Talking point:** in the winter of 2022–23 the Skateway didn't open at all; it wasn't cold enough. What does she make of that?
